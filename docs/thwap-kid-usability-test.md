# Thwap Hockey Training - Kid Usability Test

A turnkey script for testing thwaphockey.com with 3 to 5 real players aged 9 to 13 from the Vancouver Jr. Rangers 10U team.

Guiding principle (Vitaly Friedman): do not validate, research instead. You are here to watch what kids DO, not to hear what they say they like. Do not ask leading questions. Do not help unless the child is truly stuck. Getting lost is not a failure of the test; it is the finding.

## 1. Goal

Find out whether a 9 to 13 year old can open the app and complete today's training and understand the reward loop without any adult help, and whether the app feels like their hockey world. You are watching for three things at once: can they get in and do a drill on their own, does the reward loop (marking done, leaderboard, stickers) make sense to them, and does it feel like it belongs to them and their team rather than to a grown-up. If a kid needs you to point, explain, or rescue them, that is a real signal about the design, not about the kid.

## 2. Before You Start

Setup checklist:

- [ ] A phone with the site already open at thwaphockey.com, screen brightness up, notifications off.
- [ ] Parent consent, in person, because these are minors. Tell the parent exactly what you will record and why.
- [ ] Record only the screen and the child's voice, and only if the parent agrees. NEVER record the child's face. If the parent says no to any recording, just take notes.
- [ ] Keep the whole session to about 15 minutes. Stop earlier if the child is tired or restless.
- [ ] Have the child use their own name if they are on the roster. If they are not, use "Lewie" as the demo name.
- [ ] Have the tally sheet from section 7 printed or open on a second device.
- [ ] Sit beside the child, not across from them, so you can see the screen without leaning in.

## 3. How to Behave

You are a quiet observer, not a coach or a teacher. Your instinct will be to help. Fight it.

- Stay quiet. Silence is your main tool.
- When the child pauses or looks stuck, count silently to 10 before you say anything. Most kids figure it out in that window.
- Ask "What are you thinking?" or "What would you do next?" Never ask "Do you like it?" or "Was that easy?" Those questions make kids tell you what they think you want to hear.
- Never point at the screen. Never touch the phone during a task. If you point, you have solved the task for them and lost the finding.
- Let them get lost. A wrong turn, a dead end, a confused face: write it down, do not fix it.
- If a child asks "What do I do?", turn it back gently: "What do you think you could try?"
- Only step in if they are genuinely stuck after your count of 10 and are getting frustrated. When you do, note it as a "help" on the tally sheet.

## 4. The Four Tasks

For each task, read the line out loud exactly as written, then sit back and watch.

### Task A: "Show me what you have to do today"

- Say to the kid: "Show me what you have to do today."
- Success looks like: from the home screen, the child finds today's training on their own and can tell you what it is.
- Watch for: Do they know where to tap first? Do they scroll past it? Do they confuse today's training with something else on the home screen? How long before they land on it?

### Task B: "Do one of the drills and mark it done"

- Say to the kid: "Do one of the drills and mark it done."
- Success looks like: the child opens a drill, understands what the drill is asking them to do, and marks it done on their own.
- Watch for: Can they open a drill? Do they understand the drill from what is on screen? Does the mark-done control read as tappable to them, or do they hunt for it? If a PIN step appears, does it confuse or block them? Note exactly where any hesitation happens.

### Task C: "Show me the team leaderboard and tell me how the team is doing"

- Say to the kid: "Show me the team leaderboard and tell me how the team is doing."
- Success looks like: the child finds the leaderboard, reads the team goal, and can say in their own words how the team is doing.
- Watch for: Do they read the team goal, or skip straight to names and ranks? Do they immediately look for themselves and compare to others? Does ranking make them light up, or does it make them shrink or go quiet? Their body language here matters as much as their words.

### Task D: "Find a sticker you want and tell me why"

- Say to the kid: "Find a sticker you want and tell me why."
- Success looks like: the child finds the sticker book, picks a sticker they want, and gives a real reason.
- Watch for: Does the sticker book spark curiosity and desire? Do they want to keep looking? Do they ask how to earn one? Or do they shrug and move on? Genuine "I want that one" energy is the signal you are looking for.

## 5. After

Ask these open questions in kid language after the tasks are done. Let them answer fully. Do not correct them.

1. "If your friend had this app, what would you tell them it is?"
2. "What was confusing?"
3. "What was your favorite part?"
4. "Would you open this by yourself tomorrow? Why or why not?"

## 6. What Counts as a Problem

Use this rubric when you review your notes across all the kids:

- If 2 or more kids hesitate more than 10 seconds on the same step, take the same wrong path, or need help on the same step, that step is a real problem to fix.
- If only 1 kid struggles on a step, that is a maybe. Keep watching in future tests.
- Write down exact quotes. "I don't get where the drills are" is worth more than "child seemed confused."
- A kid completing a task slowly but on their own is a pass, not a problem. Speed is not the goal; independence is.
